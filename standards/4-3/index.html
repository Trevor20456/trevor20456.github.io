ISTE Coach Standard 4.3

Collaborator

Coaches establish productive relationships with educators in order to improve instructional practice and learning outcomes.

View the official ISTE Coach Standards ↗

Professional practice

Beginning with the Educator’s Priorities

Collaboration begins with understanding what another educator wants students to learn and what makes that work challenging. As a first-year social sciences teacher at Trona High School, I value support that respects teachers’ experience, time, and knowledge of their students. My work for this standard connects two parts of that process: listening carefully to educators and using their priorities to guide decisions about digital resources and instructional support.

Artifact One

Listening to Educators: Needs-Analysis Revision Record

Indicators

4.3.a — Trust and professional relationships. This artifact shows how I responded to professional feedback and incorporated educator voice into the questions I ask. 4.3.d — Support shaped around the educator. The survey connects individual needs and preferences to specific forms of instructional assistance.

Description

This artifact combines a record of revisions informed by my EDEL 590 discussion, a six-question formative-assessment survey, and a guide for translating responses into coaching decisions. It provides a focused way to identify educators’ existing practices, instructional priorities, access concerns, and preferred support.

View the needs-analysis revision record and survey →

Artifact contents

  1. Feedback and the resulting design decisions.
  2. Six questions that invite educator priorities and choices.
  3. Examples connecting responses to appropriate coaching support.

Implementation

For EDEL 590, I developed a needs-analysis focus on helping Trona teachers use brief formative assessments to identify student needs and adjust instruction. During the course discussion, a classmate asked whether my opening question would guide the selection of tools and whether it would use an open response or multiple-choice format. My instructor also asked about the format. These questions helped me examine how the information collected would influence the support I offered.

I revised the survey to combine select-all options with short written responses. The opening question separates assessment practices from the tools used to carry them out. This distinction matters because an educator may already have a useful questioning or exit-ticket routine and need help with a specific part of it. Additional questions address instructional decisions, learner access, participation barriers, and the educator’s preferred way of receiving support.

I also added a response-to-support guide. For example, difficulty interpreting assessment results points toward working through sample responses together, while preparation time may call for adapting an existing task. The guide calls for confirming that interpretation with the educator before choosing the support. This keeps the survey from becoming a checklist that assigns teachers a predetermined solution.

Impact

The revision strengthened the connection between listening and planning. The artifact now shows how a response can influence the content, format, or pace of support. Brief choices make the questions easier to navigate, while written responses give educators room to explain needs I may have overlooked. The revision record also makes my response to professional feedback visible.

This work changed how I think about helpful coaching. Asking which application a teacher wants to learn provides less direction than understanding the instructional decision they are trying to make. The artifact demonstrates growth in using professional feedback and designing an educator-centered conversation. Its contribution to Standard 4.3 is the deliberate use of educator voice to shape support.

Artifact Two

Collaborative Digital Resource Review and Coaching Guide

Indicators

4.3.b — Content selected with educators. The guide addresses cultural relevance, developmental appropriateness, and students’ interests and aspirations. 4.3.c — Evidence for tool and adoption decisions. It structures a joint comparison of resources and the evidence needed to justify their use. 4.3.d — Personalized planning and modeling. It connects an educator’s goal to a chosen form of support and a follow-up reflection.

Description

I created this five-step guide to organize a collaborative review of a digital resource around a specific instructional goal. It includes a comparison worksheet, a support plan, a follow-up record, and an illustrative history example showing how the process can be applied.

View the collaborative resource-review and coaching guide →

Artifact contents

Listen → Review → Decide → Personalize support → Follow up

A shared goal, evidence for the resource choice, a practical support plan, and a record of what changes.

Implementation

I organized the guide so that the educator’s goal comes before the resource comparison. The opening prompts ask about the existing lesson, what works, the obstacle the educator wants to address, and what successful learning would look like. These questions make the educator’s knowledge central to the review.

The comparison examines learning demands, content quality, cultural representation, accessibility, and practical requirements. I included the current approach alongside two candidate resources so that the decision has a meaningful point of comparison. The guide asks for evidence and unresolved questions, including whether the content reflects students’ interests and aspirations. Cost, account requirements, and school approval are included because those factors affect whether a resource is practical to adopt.

The support section offers modeling, joint planning, and rehearsal with feedback. I added a worked history example to make the process concrete while clearly identifying it as illustrative. The final section distinguishes changes in an educator’s planning or instruction from evidence about student learning. This gives the review a clear purpose beyond selecting a tool.

Impact

Developing the guide helped me translate collaboration into specific decisions and responsibilities. A discussion needs to identify the goal, explain the resource choice, establish what support is useful, and determine what evidence will be reviewed. The completed guide provides a consistent structure for those decisions while allowing the educator to shape the content and pace of the conversation.

The worked example also makes my instructional reasoning visible: resource selection depends on the learning task, the students, and the evidence needed to judge usefulness. The artifact demonstrates my ability to design a collaborative process that connects relevance, resource evaluation, and personalized support. Its value as a professional resource is the clear connection between an educator’s priority and each decision that follows.

Professional reflection

Overall Reflection on Standard 4.3

Managing five course preparations has made me aware of how quickly a useful idea can become another demand on a teacher’s time. Collaboration needs to make instructional work more manageable and purposeful. These artifacts helped me develop a process that begins with the educator’s priorities, makes the reasoning behind a recommendation clear, and leaves room for the educator to adapt the approach.

4.3.a — Building trust through listening

The needs-analysis discussion showed me the value of taking a colleague’s question seriously and using it to reconsider my own decisions. I strengthened the survey by making its purpose clearer and including opportunities for educators to explain their thinking. These choices support a respectful conversation because they recognize that teachers bring experience and knowledge of their classrooms. Receiving feedback through coursework is one part of my development; a sustained coaching relationship also requires listening, follow-through, and responsiveness over time.

4.3.b — Choosing relevant content together

The resource-review guide reflects my understanding that relevance involves more than an interesting topic or an appealing presentation. The content needs to fit the students’ readiness, represent people and perspectives respectfully, and connect with interests or aspirations that matter to them. An educator’s knowledge of the class is essential to those judgments. Including these questions in the guide helps make cultural relevance and developmental fit part of the selection process.

4.3.c — Justifying resource decisions with evidence

I built the comparison around the learning goal and the current instructional approach. This helps distinguish a useful resource from one that simply adds features or requires a new routine. The guide asks what students will think and do, what barriers may arise, and what evidence would support continued use. Including the option to adapt, retain the existing approach, or stop a trial makes the decision responsive to evidence. The same reasoning is necessary when recommending adoption or a purchase.

4.3.d — Personalizing instructional support

The two artifacts work together to connect an educator’s needs with the support provided. The survey identifies the priority and preferred format; the coaching guide translates those responses into a specific goal, demonstration, planning session, or rehearsal. Modeling is most useful when the reasoning behind an instructional choice is explained and the educator has an opportunity to question and adapt it. I designed the follow-up prompts to examine both the lesson and the usefulness of the support itself.

My strongest evidence for this standard is responsiveness to professional feedback and the completed design of resources for working with educators. These artifacts do not establish the effects of a completed coaching cycle or changes in student achievement. My continuing professional goal is to document that application through an agreed instructional goal, a jointly reviewed resource, a revised task, and the educator’s reflection. That evidence will help me evaluate how effectively my support strengthens another educator’s practice.

Standard 4.3 has sharpened my understanding of the coach’s role. The quality of a recommendation depends on how well I understand the educator’s goal, how clearly we examine the options, and how thoughtfully I respond to what the educator needs. These principles guide the resources I have created and the collaborative practice I am developing.
