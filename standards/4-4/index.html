ISTE Coach Standard 4.4

Learning Designer

Learning Designer focuses on helping educators create learning experiences and digital environments that respond to students’ needs and interests. My work connects a clear learning goal with student choice, accessible materials, assessment, and feedback.

Professional context

Designing for the thinking students need to do

In my senior government class at Trona High School, students need to explain how evidence supports a position and respond to a reasonable challenge. The executive-branch project gives them that work: partners investigate a question about presidential power or selection and develop a civic argument. With access to Chromebooks, they can research, organize a shared presentation, and revise their explanations.

For this standard, I developed two educator resources from that project. The design guide explains the choices behind the learning experience. The assessment toolkit adds a way to check individual understanding before the presentation. Together, they provide concrete material for a colleague to adapt. The documented stage is resource development; educator implementation and impact have not yet been established.

Artifact One

Executive Branch Digital Inquiry and Educator Design Guide

Indicators

4.4.a, 4.4.c, and 4.4.d: authentic learning with student choice, planning for learner variability, and modeling the reasoning behind a digital lesson design.

Description

This artifact brings together my October 2026 executive-branch debate materials and a new guide for adapting their design with another educator. The original materials include three presentation themes, a topic menu, a speaking guide, and a 100-point rubric. Students work in pairs, select a question and position, use two different scholarly sources, and deliver a five-minute presentation followed by two minutes of questions.

The educator guide explains why those components belong together. It includes an alignment table, an accessible text version of the student task, and decisions a colleague would make about sources, scaffolds, pacing, and participation. A public scoring table preserves the original assignment expectations, and a worked prompt revision makes the design process visible. Suggested adaptations are labeled separately from the original materials.

Implementation

I developed the original materials for my senior government class in October 2026; the source files are dated October 12. I added the educator guide on October 20. My completed work includes the public task, rubric, alignment analysis, access options, and a model of how to revise an instructional prompt. A planning session with another educator is the next stage.

The guide gives that session a clear sequence. Begin with the colleague’s learning goal, decide what evidence would demonstrate it, and adapt the task to their class. Then examine the digital workflow and participation demands. The colleague selects a support or revises a prompt and explains the reason for that decision. This keeps the educator involved in designing the lesson instead of simply receiving a finished template.

Impact

Educator impact has not yet been documented for this guide. The artifact currently demonstrates the preparation behind my coaching: an authentic civic task for 4.4.a, options for access and participation for 4.4.c, and a visible design process for 4.4.d. These are specific strengths of the resource. Their effect on another educator’s decisions needs to be examined through use.

The intended result is an educator who can adapt the design to their own learning goal and explain the choices they make. Evidence of that result could be a task revised during our conversation, the colleague’s explanation of a change, and feedback about what my support clarified. For example, changing a prompt from requesting a source to requiring an evidence-based explanation would show a meaningful design decision. A brief discussion can provide useful evidence if it records what the educator learned or changed.

Artifact Two

Digital Assessment and Feedback Toolkit

Indicators

4.4.b and 4.4.d: designing a digital check that leads to timely, targeted feedback and demonstrating how the assessment, learning goal, and next instructional step fit together.

Description

This toolkit is a new formative assessment resource for the executive-branch inquiry. It contains a checkpoint students can complete before presenting, criteria for interpreting responses, invented examples for educator practice, and feedback that asks students to make a specific revision. A worked revision illustrates the reasoning expected after feedback. An individual revision record helps the teacher examine each partner’s understanding.

The original rubric evaluates the finished presentation. This toolkit serves a different purpose within the lesson: it gives the teacher information while students still have time to use feedback. The questions, feedback criteria, and revision record are publicly available. They can be copied into an approved private digital form or document for classroom use.

Open the checkpoint, worked examples, and feedback toolkit →

Implementation

I prepared the toolkit on October 20, 2026, to address a design problem in a shared presentation: the finished deck can hide differences in the partners’ understanding, and a final score leaves no opportunity to improve that presentation. The completed resource includes the individual checkpoint, criteria, feedback examples, a suggested schedule, and a revision record. It has not yet been documented in use with an educator or class.

In the planned educator session, I model how to interpret one response and choose a priority comment. The colleague practices with a different response and explains their decision. We then choose where the checkpoint fits in their lesson and reserve time for revision. A digital form can organize individual responses for review; document comments can keep feedback beside the relevant explanation. The educator checks the revised reasoning before selecting further support.

Impact

Educator impact has not yet been documented for this toolkit. Its contribution to 4.4.b is a complete assessment-to-feedback sequence with different responses to different learning needs. A factual misunderstanding calls for a source check; an unsupported claim calls for an evidence explanation; a developed argument calls for a stronger rebuttal. The model and educator practice sequence also support my preparation for 4.4.d.

The intended effect is more precise assessment and feedback decisions. I will look for an educator’s revised checkpoint, an independently written feedback comment, and an explanation of the support they selected. An anonymized response and revision can help us examine whether the feedback addressed the learning need. That evidence would connect the resource to a change in educator practice and help me judge the effectiveness of my modeling.

Professional reflection

Overall Reflection on Standard 4.4

Preparing these resources made me look more closely at the difference between designing a useful assignment and helping another teacher design one. A clear template is a starting point. Coaching also requires listening to the educator’s goals, explaining my choices, and checking whether the educator can apply that reasoning. My current evidence shows the design work; the next step is to test that support with a colleague.

4.4.a — Meaningful choices and clear expectations

I can design an active task that gives students decisions to make while keeping the content expectations clear. In this project, students select a civic question and position, decide which evidence matters, and defend a recommendation. Accuracy about presidential power and the Constitution remains part of the rubric regardless of the position they choose. That combination gives student choice an academic purpose.

I still need to demonstrate the collaborative part of this indicator with an educator. My next step is to adapt the task together and examine where students have meaningful control over their work. The current project offers choices within a common presentation format. Depending on the colleague’s learning goal, we could consider another product that still requires an accurate explanation, evidence, and a response to a challenge.

4.4.b — Feedback students can use

My strongest evidence here is the connection between a response and the support that follows. The toolkit makes the checkpoint useful by placing it before the presentation and requiring students to revisit their reasoning. It also checks each partner individually. I can explain why these choices improve the assessment design, although I have not yet demonstrated their effectiveness through educator use.

My next step is to help a colleague use the sequence in a lesson and review whether the prompt reveals the intended understanding. I will examine the feedback they write and the instructional decision they make. If a response is too vague to interpret, the question needs revision. If a student understands the content but cannot access the response format, the support needs adjustment. The assessment should help the educator distinguish those needs.

4.4.c — Access and active participation

I can identify several barriers in this task and plan specific options for addressing them. A student may understand the civic issue but struggle with a dense article, an unclear sequence of directions, or an oral presentation. The guide separates those demands. Each partner also researches a source, explains a contribution, and completes an individual check, making participation part of the design.

My current evidence is strongest in anticipating learner variability. The next step is to review an adapted digital lesson with an educator and test its student workflow: finding directions, opening sources, navigating the materials, receiving feedback, and submitting a response. The educator’s knowledge of the class and learner feedback should guide the revisions. Access options need to work in the actual environment while preserving the learning goal.

4.4.d — Making design decisions visible

I can explain the instructional design principles behind these resources. Using McTighe and Wiggins’s (2012) backward-design sequence, I begin with the learning goal, identify the evidence students should produce, and plan experiences that prepare them. The slide prompts make parts of the argument visible. The checkpoint then creates an opportunity to improve those parts before the final performance.

The worked prompt revision gives an educator a concrete model to examine, and the toolkit provides practice in selecting feedback. My next step is to model these decisions in conversation with a colleague and ask them to apply the same reasoning to another lesson. Their explanation of how the learning goal shaped the assessment and activities will help me determine whether my modeling was useful.

How I will evaluate the coaching

I plan to retain the agreed goal, the educator’s adapted task or assessment, and a brief account of what changed through our work. Where available, an anonymized feedback-and-revision example will add evidence of how the change functioned in a lesson. These records will help me assess both the educator’s decisions and what I need to improve in my support.
