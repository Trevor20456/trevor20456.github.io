ISTE Coach Standard 4.5

Professional Learning Facilitator

I caught myself explaining too many features of Google Forms and handed my colleague the keyboard. By our third meeting, that same teacher was leading our review of student writing. This standard asks me to plan professional learning around what educators need, give them room to practice, and check what they can do with that experience.

Artifact one · 4.5.b and 4.5.c

From modeling a Google Form to handing over the review

In October 2026, I met three times with a colleague for my EDEL 590 mentoring project. They brought a writing assignment they already taught. Their students could submit answers online, but the teacher wanted to understand where the reasoning broke down. We chose one question to guide our work. Could students explain why a detail they selected supported their claim?

How we used the three meetings

At our first meeting, I modeled three prompts asking students to make a claim, choose a detail, and explain the connection. My colleague adapted the prompts and tested the Form on a Chromebook. At our Friday check-in, my colleague reported feeling more confident after focusing on one writing skill. We agreed to spend the next meeting reading students’ answers.

At our second meeting, I asked my colleague what stood out before offering my own interpretation. Students could pick a useful detail, but many repeated it when we asked why it mattered. We co-planned a model paragraph and a chance for students to revise. My colleague tried the adjustment before our Friday check-in. That sequence put the ISTE Educator Analyst standard into practice. My colleague used evidence from a digital assessment to choose feedback students could act on.

What changed for my colleague

At our third meeting, my colleague brought a new passage, led the review, and chose a small-group follow-up for students who still needed a prompt. I waited to give feedback until I heard the teacher explain why that group needed support. At our final check-in, they told me the review still took too long. We agreed to use shorter follow-ups to develop a reusable sorting routine. That request changed what I planned to offer next.

Artifact two · 4.5.a and 4.5.c

Designing professional learning around teachers’ next instructional decision

In fall 2026, I developed goals, learning objectives, and assessments for a separate project, my yearlong Technology Professional Development Plan (TPDP) for Trona’s secondary teachers. I sent a needs survey to ten colleagues and eight responded. Six of eight felt confident using basic digital tools. Six wanted support interpreting student responses and deciding what to teach next. Seven identified limited time, and six asked for collaborative planning.

What colleagues’ input changed

I built the workshop around a lesson each teacher is about to teach. After a short model, teachers will work with a partner to design a three-to-five-item check. One item must reveal a student’s reasoning, and another part of the check must give students a way to communicate confusion or ask for support. Teachers will also review the reading demands and include an accommodation or alternative way to respond. A teacher who wants help getting started can use a template. A colleague who already uses Forms can use that time to improve a question.

How I will evaluate the plan

My evaluation starts with the assessment a teacher already uses and one change they want to make. After two classroom cycles, I will compare that starting point with the revised questions, anonymized student responses, and the teacher’s decision about feedback or reteaching. My plan sets a target of at least 80% of participating teachers meeting all six objectives. If teachers design good questions but still have trouble interpreting the answers, the next meeting will focus on reading student work together. That gives us a practical way to support Trona’s goal of developing independent thinkers who can explain their reasoning.

Professional reflection

What I learned about facilitating professional learning

4.5.a · Design professional learning around adult learners

I can use a needs assessment to make specific choices about time, pacing, and practice. My colleague chose the writing lesson. In the yearlong plan, teachers bring a current lesson and choose a goal that fits their subject. These choices draw on two adult learning principles. Teachers direct part of their learning and apply their experience to an immediate classroom problem. I have more to learn about what makes it comfortable for different colleagues to ask for help. A private opening check-in about examples, language, and participation preferences would help me adapt the session to the people in the room.

4.5.b · Build educator capacity through practice and feedback

When I slowed down and let my colleague work through one question, they had time to practice a decision they would make again on their own. By our third meeting, the teacher could explain which students needed a follow-up and why. In a group session, I want every participant to have that turn. I can ask each teacher to interpret one response, choose a teaching move, and explain the choice before I offer feedback.

4.5.c · Evaluate impact and improve the next session

I have used evidence to adjust my coaching with one colleague. The Friday check-ins helped us move from building a Form to reading the responses, and my colleague’s final reflection gave us a reason to shorten the review. For the schoolwide plan, I chose a similar measure of independence. A teacher brings two checks on the same learning target and explains what changed after an instructional adjustment. Hearing that reasoning will help me decide which part of the process deserves more coaching time.
