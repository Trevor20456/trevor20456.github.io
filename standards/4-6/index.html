ISTE Coach Standard 4.6

Data-Driven Decision-Maker

Standard 4.6 asks coaches to help educators use both quantitative and qualitative evidence to guide instruction and professional learning. In our October writing review, a student could choose the right quotation and still struggle to explain why it mattered.

Professional context

Who gets to interpret the evidence?

In October 2026, a colleague at Trona wanted to know where students got stuck when they wrote about a text. We used Google Forms to look at their claims, supporting details, and explanations separately. My colleague used those responses to decide what to teach next. The Government worksheet on this page was designed to give students a turn at making that decision about their own work.

These experiences address three parts of the standard. 4.6.a asks me to model how to collect, protect, and examine student information responsibly. 4.6.b asks me to help educators interpret numbers alongside words and observations to support individual learners and judge whether an instructional change helped. 4.6.c asks me to work with educators so students can choose their own goals and use evidence to track progress.

Artifact one · 4.6.a and 4.6.b

Reading the reasoning behind a claim

I developed a response guide around a writing task my colleague chose. The three prompts in our Form separated the student’s claim, choice of evidence, and reasoning. Reading one question at a time helped us decide who needed help finding a detail, who needed practice explaining it, and who was ready for a new passage.

How we used it

In our first October meeting, my colleague built the Form and we previewed it on a Chromebook. At our next meeting, we read the answers to the explanation prompt together. Several students had already picked useful evidence. Reteaching how to find a quotation would have missed their difficulty. We planned a model paragraph and a prompt students could use to revise. The feedback acknowledged the student’s choice of evidence and asked them to explain why that particular detail mattered.

What changed

My colleague used the model and gave students time to revise. At our third meeting, they led the review of answers to a new passage. We observed clearer explanations, although some students still needed a prompt. My colleague used that observation to plan a small-group follow-up. Students who still repeated the evidence received the explanation prompt and small-group support. Those who made the connection practiced with another text.

Artifact two · 4.6.b and 4.6.c

A student chooses the goal and the measure

I created this two-page guide in January 2026 for EDEL 518A, with 12th-grade Government in mind. It introduces qualitative and quantitative evidence through a routine called Notice–Ask–Act. Students look for a pattern, ask what might explain it, and decide what to try.

View the original data guide, worksheet, and accessible text →

Using the worksheet in a student conference

A teacher can introduce the worksheet with two questions. “What do you notice in your work?” and “What would you like to do better next time?” The student chooses a goal and a way to measure it. At the next check-in, the teacher asks the student to bring an example and decide whether to keep or change the strategy.

For example, a student might notice that they explained one of three quotations in a draft. They might aim to explain two of three on the next assignment and underline the sentence connecting each quotation to their claim. When the teacher asks which explanation is strongest and why, the student can use both the count and their own writing to judge progress.

Professional reflection

What I learned from reviewing student work with a colleague

4.6.a · Collect and protect student information

I can help an educator decide what information a learning question actually calls for. Three short prompts helped us identify the writing difficulty. We agreed that any examples brought to a future meeting should have identifying information removed. I am still developing the secure-handling part of this indicator. With a colleague, I will review who can open the Form’s responses, where our working notes belong, and which identifying details to remove from examples we bring to a coaching meeting.

4.6.b · Use evidence to choose and evaluate instruction

This is the part of the standard where I have the clearest evidence of my coaching. Asking my colleague to describe what a student still needed kept the conversation focused on teaching. By the third meeting my colleague could choose a follow-up and explain why it fit the writing. In another review, I would add a simple count of explanations that connect the evidence to the claim on each passage. Reading a few explanations together would help us interpret that count and decide whether to keep or change the modeling routine.

4.6.c · Give students a role in monitoring progress

In the October writing review, my colleague made the instructional decision. Designing the Government worksheet made me think about which decisions a student can make. The teacher can help a student interpret a score, but the goal and the measure of progress should make sense to the student. To evaluate my coaching on this indicator, I would review a deidentified goal and its later check-in with the teacher. The most telling detail would be the student’s explanation of why they kept or changed a strategy.
