Shared Digital Resource Decision and Lesson Revision Record

Trevor Arcadipane · September 2026

Shared Digital Resource Decision and Lesson Revision Record · ISTE Coach Standard 4.3

Illustrative coaching case: This completed example models decisions an educator and coach can make about an upper-secondary industrialization lesson. It is a designed resource, not a record of a completed meeting, classroom trial, or measured outcome. The educator can change any proposed decision.

1. Define the instructional problem together

Working goal: Help students distinguish a source’s evidence from their assumptions, explain its perspective, and revise a historical claim after feedback. The example begins with an existing paper task that asks students to summarize industrial working conditions. A completed summary may show recall without revealing how students evaluated the sources.

The coach opens with: “What do you want to learn from students’ responses that the current assignment does not show?” The educator selects the priority: stronger evidence, clearer individual thinking, or more useful revision. If the educator’s main concern is unfamiliar content, retain the current format and address that first. Agreement on a problem must precede agreement on a platform.

Proposed success criteria: An individual response makes a defensible claim, uses a specific detail from each source, explains a difference in their purposes, and identifies something the pair cannot establish. A revision explains why the claim became more precise. The educator and coach review these criteria together before preparing materials.

2. Select and question the content

Source A — Reform photography: Lewis Wickes Hine’s photograph of Addie Card at the North Pownal Cotton Mill, Vermont, August 1910, is held by the Library of Congress. Hine documented child labor for the National Child Labor Committee. Use the image and its catalog information together; students distinguish visible details from the photographer’s description. Open the photograph and attribution.

Source B — Legislative response: Section 1 of the Keating-Owen Child Labor Act, approved September 1, 1916, restricted interstate shipment of certain goods produced using child labor. Its age and working-time provisions express a policy response. The act was subsequently invalidated by the Supreme Court in 1918; it did not permanently end child labor. Use the original transcript alongside a clearly labeled teacher paraphrase. Open the act and historical context.

Shared selection judgment: The photograph provides a concrete starting point; the legislation asks students to interpret a different kind of evidence. These are neither opposing views nor two workers’ accounts. Neither supplies Addie Card’s own explanation, a family’s budget, an employer’s testimony, or the experiences of every racial, regional, or occupational group. Students should name these gaps instead of treating two sources as a complete history.

Readiness and relevance: The educator decides whether students can handle the legal syntax and whether the pair fits the unit’s place and period. Introduce interstate commerce, reform, and inference; chunk Section 1 into the relevant provisions while retaining the full-source link. Offer connections to photography, manufacturing, journalism, public policy, or workplace design. Let students select a question that interests them; do not assume a student’s identity determines their interests or invite disclosure of family finances.

The photograph collection’s Library of Congress record lists no known publication restrictions. Preserve the source links, creator, date, and repository credit when preparing the lesson.

3. Compare the instructional options

Hold the sources, prompt, and success criteria constant when comparing formats. Ask the educator to challenge each judgment using knowledge of the class.

Adoption judgment: Use the school’s existing approved tools only after confirming the proposed use and student accounts are permitted. This example requires no new product purchase. The educator and coach still consider preparation, feedback time, device availability, and ongoing support; an existing license does not eliminate those costs. A future purchase recommendation would require an unmet instructional need, evidence from a relevant trial, verified costs, and the school’s approval process.

Two legitimate alternatives: Choose the revised paper task when a stable routine gives more time for historical discussion. Choose a shared document when the priority is comparing interpretations together; follow it with a separate individual response if individual understanding must also be assessed. The coach records the educator’s reason instead of treating the proposed digital choice as mandatory.

4. Revise the task and feedback

Before: “Describe child labor during industrialization. Summarize both sources.”

After: “How do these sources frame child labor as a public concern? Write a claim using one precise detail from the photograph and one provision from the law. Explain how a reform photograph and legislation serve different purposes. Separate observation from inference, and identify one missing perspective that could complicate your claim.”

Response structure: Claim → photograph evidence and interpretation → legislative evidence and interpretation → source limitation. A student may plan orally or with brief notes before writing. The educator chooses the support that preserves the reasoning goal.

Model feedback: If a response says that the law ended every instance of child labor, comment: “What can a law show about policymakers’ intentions, and what additional evidence would establish its effects? Narrow your claim.” This prompts reasoning without supplying a replacement paragraph.

Revision instruction: Keep the original claim, add the revised claim below it, and explain the change in two sentences. During rehearsal, the educator writes one sample comment and checks whether a learner could act on it. The coach offers an alternative only after asking what the educator wants the comment to accomplish.

5. Agree on evidence before the trial

Bounded trial: One lesson plus a short revision opportunity. Review the class’s initial and revised responses against the four success criteria, preserving several anonymized pairs with different levels of success. Also note time spent preparing, interruptions caused by the format, and whether students understood the comments.

  • Clearer claims with meaningful explanations: Continue the routine provisionally; examine whether feedback, the revised prompt, or the platform contributed.
  • Successful submissions but weak reasoning: Revise the modeling or content support. Completion alone does not establish learning.
  • Useful reasoning with repeated navigation interruptions: Simplify the digital routine or use paper while retaining the improved prompt.
  • Copied comments or superficial revisions: Confer with students about their changes and adjust the feedback before expanding use.

A small classroom trial informs the next teaching decision; it does not isolate the platform’s effect or justify a schoolwide adoption claim.

6. Preserve educator voice in the decision record

Before teaching: The educator confirms the sources, prompt, support, and format; the coach prepares the agreed model and rehearses the unfamiliar step. Both select a follow-up time and decide what evidence is reasonable to collect.

At follow-up: The educator interprets the work first. The coach asks what surprised them, checks the interpretation against examples, and identifies any disagreement. Together they record: selected approach and rationale; evidence considered; remaining uncertainty; continue, adapt, or stop; one next action; and who will complete it.

Current example decision: Prepare a limited individual-response trial, retain paper as an equivalent option, and revisit the choice after examining reasoning and revision. Classroom results and colleague feedback remain to be collected. Keep student identities out of any public record, and obtain the educator’s agreement before using their feedback as portfolio evidence.
