Standard 4.4 · Artifact One

Executive Branch Digital Inquiry

A classroom project and educator design guide for building a civic argument with evidence.

Student taskDesign decisionsAccess and participationOriginal materials

Context and use

The original materials were developed for Trevor Arcadipane’s senior government class at Trona High School in October 2026 and have been successfully shared with another educator. This educator guide was added on October 20 to make the instructional choices explicit and support further collaboration. The linked portfolio reflection describes the sharing experience; the new guide provides a structure for adapting the project together.

Original assignment: a paired presentation using two scholarly sources, a choice of executive-branch issues, and a common rubric. New educator companion: the alignment analysis, support options, co-design questions, and suggested formative checkpoint below. These additions are recommendations for a future adaptation.

Accessible text version

The student task

Choose one question with your partner. Take a position and support it with evidence. Your audience is your classmates, who will ask questions about your reasoning.

  • Should the Electoral College be replaced with a national popular vote?
  • Should Congress place stricter limits on presidential executive orders?
  • Should there be a maximum age for a president at the start of a term?
  • Should Congress place tighter limits on presidential use of force without congressional authorization?
  • Should the federal pardon power have additional limits?
  • Should a national emergency expire after 30 days unless Congress approves its continuation? This is a proposed rule to debate.

An alternative executive-branch question requires teacher approval. Explain whether you recommend a change or support the current approach. Make any proposed change specific.

  1. Research one source each. Use two different scholarly works found through Google Scholar. Read the relevant passages in context and save the author, year, publication information, page or section, and link. An official source or class notes may provide additional background.
  2. Build one shared deck. Include your position, the current rule and constitutional connection, two reasons supported by evidence, an opposing argument and response, and a recommendation with a benefit and tradeoff. Include references.
  3. Choose the presentation design. Use one of the provided themes or your own. The order and number of slides may change if the required content remains clear.
  4. Prepare to explain the argument. Use your own note cards. Present for five minutes and answer questions for two minutes. Both partners explain evidence and share the speaking time.

The original materials specify journal articles, law-review articles, or academic book chapters. Google Scholar is a search tool, and inclusion there alone does not establish source quality or peer review. Record the actual work as the source.

Educator planning

Align the goal, evidence, and activity

Learning goal: students explain an executive-branch issue, defend a position with relevant evidence, and respond to a counterargument. Their political position is not a scoring criterion.

The planning sequence follows McTighe and Wiggins’s Understanding by Design framework (2012): establish the goal, identify evidence of learning, then plan instruction.

The digital deck places the claim, evidence, explanation, and references together so the teacher can inspect the argument. Its value depends on the thinking students put into those sections. A completed slide with a copied passage still needs instructional attention.

A design conversation with a colleague

  1. Agree on the learning. Ask which content and reasoning students must demonstrate. Use the colleague’s course goals to adapt this example.
  2. Choose the evidence. Examine what would distinguish an explanation from a summary. Decide how each learner will demonstrate understanding.
  3. Plan the work. Identify source access, modeling, research time, feedback, revision, and presentation time. Set the actual schedule together.
  4. Walk through the task. Have the colleague follow the student directions, locate the response spaces, and identify a barrier before assigning it.

Assessment and follow-through

The original rubric totals 100 points: 80 shared points and 20 individual points. The table below presents all six criteria, their full-credit expectations, and their original scoring bands in a public text format. Each partner receives the shared presentation scores and their own preparation and speaking scores.

Scoring guide (shared / individual): 20 / 10 = complete and accurate; 16 / 8 = minor gaps; 12 / 6 = partial, needs detail; 8 / 4 = major gaps or errors; missing work = 0.

The new Digital Assessment and Feedback Toolkit adds a formative check before grading. It leaves the existing 100-point rubric intact. The educator can use the check to identify one priority for revision before the presentation.

To build on the initial sharing, adapt the design with a colleague and retain the agreed learning goal, the adapted task, and the educator’s explanation of a change. Ask: “Which design decision can you now apply to another lesson?” and “Where did the materials still make participation difficult?” Review these records for evidence that the colleague can apply the design principles independently.
