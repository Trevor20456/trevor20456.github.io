Standard 4.4 · Educator resource

Digital Assessment and Feedback Toolkit

A short assessment sequence for the Executive Branch Debate: identify a gap, give useful feedback, and let students revise before presenting.

Purpose and use

This resource adds formative checkpoints to the existing senior government project. Pairs still research two distinct scholarly works, develop one position, explain two reasons, answer an objection, and present for five minutes followed by two minutes of questions. The original 100-point rubric remains the grading instrument; these checks carry no additional points.

Implementation stage: Prepared in October 2026 for adaptation with educators. All practice responses are synthetic; educator implementation and outcomes have not yet been documented.

Use an approved private digital form or document to review individual responses, return comments, and retain revisions. A paper response or conference can serve the same goal when needed. Technology organizes the exchange; the teacher evaluates reasoning and source accuracy.

Suggested schedule

  1. During drafting: Reserve about 10 minutes for the individual checkpoint, with sources and draft slides open.
  2. Before the next work period: Review responses and return one priority comment per student. Identify any common misconception for a brief explanation.
  3. Before presentations: Allow about 10 minutes for revision and three minutes for the independent exit response. Check unresolved gaps before final assessment.

Adjust the pacing with the educator. If feedback arrives late, preserve revision time by adjusting the presentation schedule.

Student checkpoint: before the presentation

Complete this check individually after your pair has drafted its evidence slides. Use your actual sources and slide references. Your teacher will identify one revision to complete before presenting.

  1. State the position. What does your pair recommend? Identify what would change or stay the same. In a separate sentence, explain the relevant current rule or power and cite the background source you checked.
  2. Explain one piece of evidence. Give the author, year, page or section, and working link for the scholarly work you researched. Summarize one relevant argument, finding, or example. Explain how it supports your pair’s position.
  3. Test the argument. State a reasonable objection. Explain your response using evidence from one of your sources. Identify one limitation or tradeoff your recommendation still needs to address.
  4. Choose a revision. Which slide or explanation most needs improvement? Name the exact change you will make and the help, if any, you need.

A sentence starter or an accessible recorded response with a text outline can support participation. Keep the reasoning, source information, and criteria consistent. Checkpoint accommodations do not automatically change the original presentation expectations.

Practice giving feedback

These first three examples illustrate reasoning about proposed limits on executive orders. Check source-dependent statements against students’ actual research.

1. A position without support

Practice response: “Congress should add limits because presidents have too much power.”

Feedback: “Name the limit you propose. Then identify one relevant argument or example in your source and explain how that evidence supports this particular limit.”

Student action: Make the claim specific and connect it to traceable evidence.

2. Evidence mentioned, reasoning incomplete

Practice response: “Our source discusses accountability. That proves our proposed limit is best.”

Feedback: “Explain the source’s actual argument. Why does it support your chosen limit? Replace ‘proves’ if the source does not compare your proposal with the alternatives.”

Student action: Revise the explanation to match what the source supports and acknowledge its limits.

3. A developed argument ready for challenge

Practice response: “Our source’s argument about accountability supports a review requirement. A review could also slow action, so our proposal needs a clear procedure for urgent situations.”

Feedback: “Explain how your proposed procedure addresses the objection. Identify what your source supports and what is your own recommendation.”

Student action: Strengthen the rebuttal and distinguish evidence from inference.

Full worked revision: checking a research plan

Source for this practice: The original project requires two distinct scholarly works, one researched by each partner. Its research guidance explains that inclusion in Google Scholar does not guarantee peer review.

Before: “We found a PDF and a website version of the same article on Google Scholar. That gives us two peer-reviewed sources, so our research is finished.”

Teacher feedback: “Count distinct works rather than links. What does the assignment require each partner to research? Explain what you still need to check about the publication.”

After: “Our two links lead to one article, so we currently have only one scholarly work. One partner will research a different journal article, law-review article, or academic book chapter. We will inspect each work’s author, publication, relevant passage, and usefulness to our argument. We cannot call a source peer-reviewed simply because Google Scholar lists it.”

Why this revision meets the criterion: The student identifies the precise gap, uses the assignment to justify a next step, and corrects an unsupported assumption. The teacher would still review the selected second work before confirming that the research requirement is met.

Independent exit response: “What changed in your reasoning, and why does the revision better meet the criterion? Explain without your partner answering for you.”

Review the revision for substantive improvement. A resolved comment or completed submission does not establish understanding. If the gap remains, provide another example or a short conference, then ask for an independent explanation.

Adapt the toolkit with an educator

Co-design: Agree on the learning goal, select the most useful checkpoint, and decide when feedback and revision can occur. Keep the checkpoint aligned with the existing assignment and rubric.

Model and rehearse: Explain how one practice response reveals a gap. Write one actionable comment, then ask the educator to assess a different response and explain their feedback choice. Compare judgments against the criteria.

Transfer and review: Have the educator adapt the sequence to another lesson. After use, retain the agreed goal, revised assessment prompt, anonymized feedback-and-revision example, and the educator’s account of what they changed. Ask whether the support improved their assessment decisions and what needs revision. These records would document impact; the toolkit alone does not.
